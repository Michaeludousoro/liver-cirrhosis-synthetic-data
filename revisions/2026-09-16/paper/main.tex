% Abstract
% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{abstract}
Limited access to complete clinical datasets motivates synthetic data generation, but statistical resemblance alone may not establish predictive value. This study tests the hypothesis that quality-filtered synthetic records improve mortality prediction on held-out real patients with primary biliary cirrhosis. Of 418 Mayo Clinic records, 276 complete cases were divided into 193 training and 83 test patients. Three core models---a generative adversarial network, a conditional GAN (cGAN), and a variational autoencoder---were combined with interquartile-range filtering and cross-model consensus. Additional comparisons included a masked-loss VAE, CTGAN, and SMOTE. Evaluation distinguished distributional fidelity, nearest-neighbour disclosure-risk indicators, and predictive utility under binary classification, three-year classification, and survival modelling. Follow-up duration was excluded from baseline predictors. The masked-loss VAE achieved the lowest tabular Fr\'{e}chet distance (0.062); augmentation increased Random Forest AUC from 0.832 to 0.868, although the paired accuracy comparison was not significant (McNemar $p=0.125$). Synthetic-only cGAN training achieved AUCs of 0.809 and 0.849 for Random Forest and logistic regression, respectively. Consensus filtering produced a 21.1\% near-duplicate rate and did not establish a consistent predictive advantage. Across six synthetic-data methods, the association between fidelity and mean synthetic-only AUC was inconclusive (Pearson $r=0.35$, $p=0.50$). These findings support evaluating synthetic clinical data against its intended task rather than selecting generators by fidelity alone. Larger external cohorts and fully nested validation are needed to establish generalisable augmentation benefits and assess disclosure risk.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Clinical prediction models require representative patient data, yet small cohorts and incomplete measurements can limit their development and evaluation. Liver cirrhosis provides a relevant setting: previous studies have examined mortality prediction and cirrhosis identification using electronic health records \cite{guo2021,obeid2023,kanwal2020}. The Mayo Clinic primary biliary cirrhosis (PBC) dataset contains 418 patients, but only 276 have complete measurements \cite{fleming1991,ucipbc}. This reduction motivates methods that can use limited clinical information more effectively.

Synthetic data generation learns patterns from observed records and samples new records from the fitted model. GANs and VAEs offer flexible approaches to this task \cite{goncalves2020,gonzales2023,yan2022,nikolentzos2023}. Their outputs may support model development when access to real records is constrained, but synthetic origin alone establishes neither anonymity nor clinical validity. Data quality must therefore be evaluated in relation to the intended use.

Two questions are distinct: whether synthetic records resemble a source cohort, and whether they improve prediction for unseen real patients. A third question concerns the potential disclosure of information about training patients. Research on synthesis quality in the \textit{Journal of Data and Information Quality} advocates a structured evaluation of multiple quality dimensions \cite{stenger2024}. In \textit{ACM Transactions on Computing for Healthcare}, Mamun et al.\ use CTGAN augmentation and sample filtering within a neonatal prediction framework \cite{mamun2026}. These studies motivate examining how quality controls relate to downstream clinical tasks; neither establishes that the same controls will improve prediction in a small PBC cohort.

This study tests whether statistical outlier filtering and cross-architecture consensus improve the predictive value of synthetic PBC records. A fixed split allocates 193 complete cases to training and 83 to held-out testing. Three core generators---Vanilla GAN, cGAN, and VAE---are evaluated individually and through consensus filtering. A masked-loss VAE, CTGAN implementation, and SMOTE provide complementary comparisons. The PBC data comprise baseline clinical measurements with follow-up outcomes, not wearable sensor streams. Consequently, connected-health and Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) applications motivate possible future use but are not evaluated deployments.

The study makes four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A comparison of five generative models and one consensus-derived dataset, with explicit separation of augmentation and synthetic-only training.
\item A reproducible quality-control procedure combining training-derived Tukey fences with nearest-neighbour agreement across three generator architectures.
\item Joint evaluation of distributional fidelity, empirical near-duplicate rates, and predictive performance on held-out real patients.
\item Sensitivity analyses using three-year outcome classification, time-to-event modelling, and partially observed training records.
\end{enumerate}
The contribution is an empirical assessment of when quality indicators agree or conflict, rather than a claim of universally improved prediction. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work; Section~\ref{sec:methodology} describes the methods; Section~\ref{sec:results} presents results and limitations; and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Synthetic Data Generation for Healthcare}

Synthetic patient data has been investigated as a means of supporting analysis when access to clinical records is constrained \cite{goncalves2020,gonzales2023,guillaudeux2023}. Evaluation must distinguish resemblance to the source data, utility for a specified task, and disclosure risk. These properties need not improve together, and synthetic generation does not by itself guarantee privacy.

On the other hand, classic augmentation methods have its own limitations. For example, synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE) builds new minority-class samples by interpolating between existing records, so it cannot produce anything outside the range already present in the data \cite{kumari2024}, \cite{figueira2022}. Rule-based and statistical generators are easier to interpret but often miss the complex, non-linear relationships between clinical biomarkers. In contrast, deep generative models handle these limitations better, since they learn those relationships directly from the data, and they are now one of the most common ways to generate synthetic clinical tabular data \cite{yan2022}, \cite{nikolentzos2023}.

\subsection{GAN Architectures for Tabular Clinical Data}

GANs work by training a generator and a discriminator against each other \cite{vega2020}. The generator tries to produce realistic synthetic records, while the discriminator attempts to distinguish synthetic records from real ones. In the context of healthcare application, previous studies have shown that GAN-generated data can improve risk prediction using electronic health records \cite{che2017}. Choi et al.\ proposed medGAN for generating multi-label patient records, showing that GAN-based approaches can be useful for clinical data synthesis \cite{choi2017}. However, standard GANs often experience mode collapse and unstable training when applied to tabular clinical datasets containing a mixture of continuous variables, binary indicators, and ordinal disease-stage variables \cite{lenz2021}, \cite{espinosa2023}.

In contrast, the cGAN has been developed to reduce some of these problems by conditioning both the generator and the discriminator on a class label, which helps ensure that minority classes are represented more fairly during training \cite{vega2020}, \cite{sharma2024}. While VAE uses a different strategy; instead of adversarial training, it learns a latent representation of patient records through an encoder-decoder structure, and new patient records are generated at inference time by sampling from that latent space \cite{nikolentzos2023}.

Other researchers have demonstrated that synthetic patient cohorts can support research in diseases where the number of available real patients is very small \cite{damico2023}. Synthetic augmentation has also been reported to improve survival prediction in rare-disease tabular datasets similar in nature to the PBC dataset used in this study \cite{kim2024}. In addition, high-quality synthetic patient data has been shown to be useful for testing healthcare software systems, provided that the quality of the generated data is carefully evaluated \cite{tucker2020}.

\subsection{From Distributional Fidelity to Predictive Utility}

Most of the approaches so far focus on distributional fidelity, how closely synthetic data resembles real data, using metrics like Fr\'{e}chet Inception Distance (FID), Jensen-Shannon divergence, or Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistics \cite{yan2022}, \cite{figueira2022}, \cite{dankar2022}. However good distributional similarity does not guarantee good predictive performance. Espinosa and Figueira found the link between distributional metrics and downstream utility is often weak \cite{espinosa2023}. Foraker et al.\ reported that synthetic data can give different analytical results than the real data it was built from, even when it looks highly realistic \cite{foraker2020}. Kababji et al.\ argued a more meaningful test is whether models trained on synthetic data can reproduce clinically important findings from real patients \cite{kababji2023}. Kaabachi et al.\ pointed to a lack of evaluation on fully held-out real patients as a major weakness across many medical synthetic-data studies \cite{kaabachi2023}. The main question about following the path from distributional fidelity to predictive utility has received much less attention, especially for small tabular clinical datasets used in IoMT environments. \cite{kababji2023}, \cite{espinosa2023}, and that is the main motivation of this study to test predictive utility on unseen real patients, not just similarity metrics.

\subsection{Multi-Model Ensemble Strategy}

Some previous studies have explored the idea of combining the outputs of multiple generative models. Jiang et al.\ proposed a multi-source augmentation framework in which several GAN models are trained independently and their outputs are combined to improve data quality \cite{jiang2023}. The consensus voting method used in this study is related to that idea, but differs in two important ways. First, it uses three different generative architectures rather than several versions of the same GAN. Second, a synthetic record is accepted only when there is agreement across all three models, instead of simply merging all generated records together.

Pandey et al.\ examine conditional synthetic generation in an IoMT setting \cite{pandey2025}. The present study instead evaluates baseline tabular clinical records and follow-up outcomes. Its consensus procedure tests whether proximity across independently generated pools provides a useful selection criterion. The results identify circumstances in which statistical agreement is insufficient to establish downstream benefit, providing evidence for task-specific quality assessment.

Two studies provide complementary methodological context. Stenger et al.\ organise synthesis-quality assessment for time series \cite{stenger2024}; the present work applies a multidimensional evaluation perspective to a static clinical table with survival outcomes. Mamun et al.\ combine CTGAN augmentation with filtering for neonatal outcome prediction and explanation \cite{mamun2026}; the present study examines a different disease cohort and explicitly contrasts fidelity, augmentation, synthetic-only utility, and empirical disclosure indicators.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}

The Mayo Clinic PBC dataset is obtained from the UCI Machine Learning Repository \cite{fleming1991,ucipbc}. After removal of the patient identifier, it contains 19 variables: 17 baseline covariates, follow-up duration (N\_Days), and outcome status. The 11 continuous variables comprise follow-up duration, age, bilirubin, cholesterol, albumin, copper, alkaline phosphatase (Alk\_Phos), serum glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase (SGOT), triglycerides, platelets, and prothrombin. Age and follow-up duration are not laboratory biomarkers. Five variables are binary (sex, treatment, ascites, hepatomegaly, and spiders), and two are ordinal (edema and histological stage). Follow-up duration is an outcome-related variable, not a baseline predictor.

Complete-case selection retains 276 of 418 records. The excluded records include 106 observational-cohort patients with extensive missing measurements and 36 patients with fewer missing fields. Complete-case analysis avoids specifying an imputation model but can introduce selection bias; additional analyses therefore examine partially observed records. Binary variables are encoded as sex (female~=~0, male~=~1), treatment (placebo~=~0, D-penicillamine~=~1), and clinical signs (absent~=~0, present~=~1). Edema categories N, S, and Y are mapped to 0, 1, and 2; stage remains coded 1--4. For binary classification, status is encoded as deceased~=~1 and censored/transplanted~=~0. This binary endpoint does not account for differences in follow-up and is supplemented by fixed-horizon and survival analyses.

A stratified 70:30 split with random seed 42 produces 193 training patients and 83 held-out test patients. The training partition contains 115 censored/transplanted patients and 78 deaths. The min-max scaler used for generation is fitted on the training partition only. N\_Days remains a generated variable for outcome construction and survival analysis, but all predictive models use only the 17 baseline covariates.

Figure~\ref{fig:raincloud} displays the distributions of selected biomarkers in the real training partition. The half-violin density, box plot, and individual observations show skewness and between-patient variability that summary means can obscure. The figure is descriptive and does not use test patients to set filtering thresholds.

\subsection{Generative Model Architectures}

All three models are implemented from scratch in TensorFlow 2.15 without third-party synthetic data libraries. Each model receives the min-max scaled training data and produces samples in the same normalised $[0, 1]$ range, which are inverse-transformed to recover original clinical units after generation. Each model generates 500 synthetic records.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item {\textbf {Vanilla GAN:}} The generator maps a 100-dimensional noise vector $\mathbf{z}$ drawn from a standard normal distribution through two dense hidden layers of 128 and 256 units with leaky ReLU activations ($\alpha = 0.2$) and batch normalisation (momentum~$= 0.8$) to a sigmoid output of dimension 18. The discriminator maps each patient record through two dense layers of 256 and 128 units with leaky ReLU activations and dropout ($p = 0.3$). Both networks use the Adam optimiser with learning rate $2 \times 10^{-4}$ and $\beta_1 = 0.5$ for 500 epochs at batch size 32. The adversarial objectives are:
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_D &= -\mathbb{E}[\log D(\mathbf{x})] - \mathbb{E}[\log(1 - D(G(\mathbf{z})))] \\
\mathcal{L}_G &= -\mathbb{E}[\log D(G(\mathbf{z}))]
\end{align}

where $G$ is the generator, $D$ is the discriminator, $\mathbf{x}$ denotes a real training record, and $\mathbf{z}$ is a sampled noise vector. Expectations are taken over the real-data and latent-noise distributions, respectively.

\item {\textbf {cGAN:}} Both generator and discriminator are conditioned on the outcome label $\mathbf{y}$, represented as a two-dimensional one-hot vector, following Mirza and Osindero \cite{mirza2014}. The generator receives $[\mathbf{z}\|\mathbf{y}]$ and the discriminator receives $[\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y}]$. Architecture and optimiser settings match the Vanilla GAN; training uses balanced mini-batches and 300 epochs. This implementation differs from CTGAN \cite{xu2019}: it does not include mode-specific normalisation, training-by-sampling, or a packed Wasserstein critic. A separate CTGAN comparison is reported in Section~\ref{sec:ctgancomp}.

\item {\textbf {VAE:}} A standard variational autoencoder is fitted to the min-max-scaled table. It does not implement the mode-specific preprocessing of TVAE \cite{xu2019}; the label VAE therefore identifies the architecture evaluated here. The encoder maps each patient record through two dense layers of 128 and 64 units with ReLU activations to a mean vector $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and log-variance vector $\log(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2)$ of dimension 32. A latent sample is drawn using the reparameterisation trick:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \cdot \exp(0.5 \cdot \log(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2)),
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ is drawn from a standard normal. The decoder maps $\mathbf{z}$ through two dense layers of 64 and 128 units with ReLU activations to a sigmoid output of dimension 18. The training objective is the evidence lower bound (ELBO):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{VAE}} = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}\|^2\right] + \beta \cdot D_{\text{KL}}\!\left(\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\sigma}^2) \,\|\, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})\right)
\end{equation}
with $\beta = 1.0$, Adam optimiser at learning rate $1 \times 10^{-3}$, batch size 32, and 300 training epochs. Training loss curves for all three models are given in Fig.~S2 in the supplementary file. The loss curves describe optimisation behaviour; they do not establish convergence or exclude mode collapse.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{IQR-Based Statistical Outlier Filter}

Tukey's inner fences are used to flag statistical outliers \cite{nistfences}. For each of the 11 continuous variables, quartiles $Q_1$ and $Q_3$ are estimated from real training data and $\mathrm{IQR}=Q_3-Q_1$. A generated record is retained only if every continuous value lies within $[Q_1-1.5\,\mathrm{IQR},Q_3+1.5\,\mathrm{IQR}]$. Binary and ordinal variables are mapped to valid categories during post-processing. These empirical fences are not clinical reference ranges: an excluded value may represent a genuine severe case. Filtering can therefore truncate clinically relevant tails as well as remove synthetic outliers.

Applied to 500 generated records per model, the filter retained 331 GAN records (66.2\%), 292 cGAN records (58.4\%), and 499 VAE records (99.8\%). Fig.~S13 in the supplementary file illustrates the effect on the Bilirubin distribution, the most challenging continuous feature to reproduce effectively.

\subsection{Multi-Method Consensus Voting}

Consensus is defined as local agreement between synthetic pools, rather than agreement between predicted labels. Let $S_1,S_2,S_3$ denote the filtered outputs of the three core generators. A candidate $\mathbf{x}\in S_i$ is retained only if each other pool contains at least one neighbouring record within a common distance tolerance. Neighbours need not be identical or describe the same patient. Unlike simply pooling multiple generators \cite{jiang2023}, this rule requires representation of the candidate's local feature region in all three architectures.

Each feature is standardised as $z_j=(x_j-\mu_j)/s_j$, where $\mu_j$ and $s_j$ are its mean and standard deviation in the combined filtered synthetic pools. The implementation uses scikit-learn's \texttt{StandardScaler} \cite{pedregosa2011}. For each candidate record from one model, the minimum Euclidean distance to any record from each of the other two models is computed in standardised space. The candidate is accepted if both other models have a record within tolerance~$= 5.0$ standardised units. This process repeats with each filtered dataset as the candidate pool in turn, and exact duplicates are removed from the final set. The threshold of 5.0 was selected on the basis of a systematic sensitivity analysis conducted across eight values from 2.5 to 6.0. At thresholds of 3.0 and below, the accepted set contains around 10\% deceased patients or fewer, a severe class imbalance relative to the real training cohort (40\% deceased). As the tolerance increases the deceased fraction recovers (24.7\% at 5.0) and the FID score falls monotonically (0.095 at 5.0), after which further increases yield only marginal FID improvements (0.0864 at 5.5, 0.0829 at 6.0) while admitting records with progressively weaker cross-model agreement. The value of 5.0 is an empirical compromise between retention and cross-model proximity; it does not restore the real outcome proportion or establish an optimal threshold. To address the pool-size imbalance across the three filtered datasets (GAN 331 records, cGAN 292 records, VAE 499 records), the VAE pool is randomly downsampled to 292 records before voting, matching the size of the smallest adversarial pool. This equalisation gives the two higher-fidelity adversarial models a proportionally stronger role in the consensus. The equalised consensus of 787 records achieves FID 0.0809, with source composition GAN 33.2\%, cGAN 29.7\%, and VAE 37.1\%.

\subsection{Synthetic Data Quality Evaluation}

Distributional fidelity is summarised by a Gaussian Fr\'{e}chet distance, retained under the label FID for consistency with the result files. It is calculated on the 11 continuous variables, using min-max scaling fitted to real training data; no Inception network is used:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FID}=\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_r-\boldsymbol{\mu}_s\|^2+
\mathrm{Tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_r+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_s
-2(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_r\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_s)^{1/2}\right).
\end{equation}
Here $\boldsymbol{\mu}_r,\boldsymbol{\mu}_s$ are real and synthetic mean vectors, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_r,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_s$ are covariance matrices, and $\mathrm{Tr}$ denotes the matrix trace. Lower values indicate closer first and second moments, not necessarily matching joint distributions or predictive value.

Per-variable Kolmogorov--Smirnov tests assess differences between empirical continuous distributions. Histogram-based Jensen--Shannon divergence summarises distributional dissimilarity, while Cohen's $d$ measures standardised mean differences. Shapiro--Wilk tests describe normality in the real data, and chi-square tests compare categorical frequencies. These complementary measures distinguish mean agreement from distributional shape; the multiple per-feature tests are exploratory.

Empirical disclosure risk is screened using nearest-neighbour distances across the 18 generated non-status variables, including N\_Days. For this analysis, means and standard deviations are fitted to real training patients, then applied to both real and synthetic records \cite{pedregosa2011}. A synthetic record is flagged when its nearest-real distance is below the fifth percentile of leave-one-out real-to-real nearest-neighbour distances. The resulting near-duplicate rate is a proximity indicator, not a probability of re-identification or a formal privacy guarantee.

\subsection{Predictive Utility Evaluation}
\label{sec:predutil}

N\_Days records time from registration to death, transplantation, or censoring. It is unavailable at baseline and is excluded from all predictor inputs. Generators retain it as an outcome-related quantity so that synthetic follow-up and status can define three-year labels or time-to-event targets. The same separation between baseline predictors and outcome information is applied to real and synthetic records.

Predictive utility is evaluated by training random forest (RF), gradient boosting (GB), and logistic regression (LR) classifiers under the following five training scenarios and testing on the 83-patient held-out real test set:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Scenario A:} 193 real patients only (baseline).
  \item \textbf{Scenario B:} 193 real + IQR-filtered cGAN records. cGAN is selected here because it achieves the lowest FID among the three individual trained generators (refer section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item \textbf{Scenario C:} 193 real + consensus records.
  \item \textbf{Scenario D:} 193 real patients augmented with SMOTE oversampling. Scenario A is the primary real-only reference; Scenario D is an established oversampling comparator used to assess whether generative methods offer benefits beyond interpolation.
  \item \textbf{Scenario E:} IQR-filtered synthetic records only, with no real patients in training (synthetic-only, train-on-synthetic-test-on-real). Section~\ref{sec:sixgen} extends this scenario from cGAN alone to all six synthetic-data methods available in this study (Vanilla GAN, cGAN, VAE, a masked-loss VAE trained on a larger partially observed pool, a full CTGAN implementation, and consensus), so that FID can be checked against downstream utility across more than one point.
\end{enumerate}
The classifiers use fixed configurations across scenarios: RF and GB have 200 estimators, GB has maximum depth 3, and LR allows 1000 iterations. Metrics include accuracy, weighted F1, weighted precision, weighted recall, and AUC-ROC. Bootstrap intervals use 1000 resamples of the held-out test patients. McNemar's exact test compares paired classification errors; it does not test differences in AUC.

Five-fold stratified validation partitions only the real training patients. However, the current implementation reuses synthetic pools generated from the full 193-patient training partition. Thus synthetic rows are absent from validation, but generator training has seen validation-fold patients. These cross-validation results are exploratory and require regeneration of synthetic pools within each fold for an independent estimate. The 83-patient test partition remains separate from generator fitting. Random seeds are fixed at 42 for Python, NumPy, and TensorFlow. The recorded project environment specifies Python 3.9.6 and TensorFlow 2.15; package versions are listed in the accompanying requirements file.

\subsection{Fixed-Horizon Classification}
\label{sec:landmark}

A three-year endpoint is used to reduce ambiguity from unequal follow-up in the binary status outcome. Death before 1,095.75 days is labelled an event; observed survival to that horizon is labelled a non-event, irrespective of later outcome. Patients censored or transplanted before the horizon are excluded because their death status at that horizon is not observed. This exclusion does not eliminate potential selection bias from censoring. The same rule is applied to real and synthetic records. It retains 187 of 193 training patients and 76 of 83 test patients. Scenarios A--E use the same 17 baseline predictors. The term fixed-horizon classification is used because this analysis does not introduce a later prediction landmark with updated covariates.

\subsection{Time-to-Event Survival Modelling}
\label{sec:survival}

A Cox proportional-hazards model with ridge penalty 0.1 and a random survival forest (RSF; 200 trees, minimum leaf size 3) use N\_Days as duration, status as event indicator, and the same 17 baseline covariates. Harrell's concordance index (C-index) is evaluated on held-out real patients. These models use observed follow-up time rather than assigning every censored patient the same binary prognosis.

Death is the event of interest; patients are removed from the risk set at transplantation or other censoring. The Cox analysis estimates a cause-specific death hazard and does not directly estimate death probability in the presence of transplantation. Figure~S11 reports Aalen--Johansen cumulative incidence estimates with transplantation treated as a competing event. The 276 complete cases include 111 deaths and 18 transplants. These event counts alone cannot establish that competing-risk effects are negligible. Fine--Gray regression or an explicit cause-specific cumulative-incidence analysis would be needed to examine that question; neither is reported here.

Scenarios A--E parallel the classification experiments. For Scenario D, an exploratory event-class interpolation procedure generates records between same-class neighbours in standardised covariate space. It applies the same interpolation weight to the covariates and duration, retaining event~=~1 until event and censored classes are balanced. This procedure is not standard SMOTE and has not been independently validated for survival outcomes.

Kaplan--Meier curves and exploratory log-rank tests compare synthetic and real training follow-up distributions, with transplantation censored. These comparisons supplement marginal fidelity metrics; they do not establish equivalent survival distributions when a test is non-significant.

\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Distributional Fidelity}

Among the three core generators, cGAN has the lowest FID (0.0724), followed by GAN (0.0995) and VAE (0.2280). Consensus achieves 0.0809. \subsection{Discussion}

The held-out comparisons show model-dependent changes rather than a consistent augmentation advantage. McNemar's tests do not establish differences in classification accuracy at the 0.05 threshold; they do not establish equivalence or test the AUC differences. The small cohort and multiple comparisons limit the strength of both positive and negative conclusions.

Cross-validation provides exploratory context but cannot independently confirm the generative comparisons because the synthetic pools were fitted before fold partitioning. SMOTE is fitted within each fold and yields a small descriptive increase in RF mean AUC. A fully nested comparison is required to evaluate whether either generative augmentation or interpolation provides a reproducible benefit.

\label{sec:power}
Figure~\ref{fig:power} reports a sensitivity calculation using observed discordant-pair rates. Under its assumptions, a 5-percentage-point accuracy gain has low estimated power in the 83-patient test cohort, and approximately 270 held-out patients would be needed for 80\% power. This conditional calculation is not proof of a true effect or a general sample-size requirement. Non-significant paired tests leave small benefits and harms unresolved.

Scenario~E in Table~\ref{tab:performance} questions whether a classifier trained only on synthetic data can generalise to real patients, on the leak-free feature set. Using the 292 IQR-filtered cGAN records as the only training data, and the held-out 83 real patients as the test set, Logistic Regression discriminates best at AUC~$=0.849$, followed by RF at AUC~$=0.809$. Supplementary Table~S8 compares Scenarios A and C by disease stage (stages 1--2, $n=17$; stages 3--4, $n=66$), sex (female, $n=72$; male, $n=11$), and treatment (D-penicillamine, $n=39$; placebo, $n=44$). Differences vary by classifier and subgroup. The small early-stage and male groups yield particularly unstable estimates; none of these descriptive comparisons establishes subgroup equivalence or fairness. External validation with adequate subgroup sample sizes is required.

The output-perturbation sweep is shown in supplementary Fig.~S15 and Table~S6. Gaussian noise is added independently to each of the 11 continuous VAE variables, with standard deviation $\sigma s_j$, where $s_j$ is the corresponding real-training standard deviation; negative perturbed values are clipped to zero. At $\sigma=0.7$, the reported near-duplicate rate falls from 35.7\% to 1.4\%, while the tabular FID falls from 0.228 to 0.0481. This indicates a change in proximity and moment matching, not demonstrated protection against an adversary. Downstream utility and clinical validity after perturbation require separate assessment. The DP-SGD values in supplementary Table~S7 are approximate analytical projections from a simplified privacy-accounting calculation, not results from differentially private model training or certified privacy guarantees.

\subsection{Interpreting the Effect of Augmentation}
\label{sec:whynot}

A generator fitted to a fixed training cohort resamples a learned representation of that cohort. It does not add independent patient observations, but it can change the effective training distribution, regularise a model, or alter class balance. These mechanisms can help or harm prediction depending on the generator, classifier, and task. The present results therefore support an empirical interpretation rather than a theorem that synthetic augmentation cannot improve accuracy.

Consensus favours regions represented by all three generator architectures. The resulting concentration may reduce diversity or underrepresent rare outcomes, consistent with the observed class imbalance and near-duplicate rate. However, neither proximity nor the current experiments prove that all accepted records are redundant or that only decision-boundary samples can improve learning. These explanations remain hypotheses for further analysis.

Synthetic-only cGAN training yields AUC 0.809 for RF and 0.849 for LR. This demonstrates that the generated data preserve some task-relevant structure in the present split. It does not establish equivalence to real-data training, cross-institution transfer, or a sufficient sample size beyond which augmentation cannot help.

\subsection{Implications for Connected-Health Research}
\label{sec:federated}

Synthetic-only prediction motivates further investigation of data sharing for connected-health research. However, this experiment uses a single historical cohort; it does not evaluate federated learning, transfer between hospitals, wearable streams, communication costs, or deployment governance. The reported AUCs cannot be attributed to a receiving hospital or an external patient population.

A future multisite study should test transportability, calibration, subgroup performance, and privacy attacks under the intended access conditions. Near-duplicate screening and output noise can inform such an assessment but do not establish anonymous data release. Generator selection should account for the downstream classifier: the low cGAN GB AUC is not a universal result across generators, as Vanilla GAN and VAE yield GB AUCs of 0.8297 and 0.8152, respectively.

\subsection{Comparison Against a Full CTGAN Implementation}
\label{sec:ctgancomp}

The cGAN follows label-conditioned adversarial learning \cite{mirza2014}. A separate implementation incorporating CTGAN's mode-specific normalisation, conditional training-by-sampling, and packed Wasserstein critic \cite{xu2019} provides an additional comparison. Architecture settings are documented in the supplementary material.

At the evaluated budget of 4,000 updates, CTGAN reaches FID 0.108 and retains 40.8\% of generated records after filtering, compared with 0.072 and 58.4\% for cGAN. These values favour cGAN on these two criteria in this run. Differences in optimisation and training budget prevent a general conclusion about architectural superiority. CTGAN remains included in the six-method synthetic-only comparison.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}

The study uses one historical PBC cohort, one held-out split, and fixed model settings. Complete-case selection removes 142 patients, including 106 from an observational cohort with extensive missingness. Selection and cohort differences may limit generalisation; the complete-case results do not represent all patients with cirrhosis.

A masked-loss VAE uses observed fields from 335 training patients (193 complete plus 142 partial records). Its augmentation yields RF, GB, and LR AUCs of 0.868, 0.846, and 0.824, respectively, compared with real-only values of 0.832, 0.830, and 0.842. For RF, six previously incorrect predictions become correct and one previously correct prediction becomes incorrect; McNemar's exact $p=0.125$ does not establish an accuracy improvement. The additional real records confound a direct architecture-only comparison, and the small test cohort leaves considerable uncertainty.

Using only the 36 incomplete same-protocol records provides a more restricted sensitivity analysis. Median-imputed inclusion changes AUC by $+0.004$, $+0.001$, and $+0.007$ for RF, GB, and LR. A masked-loss VAE fitted to the 229-patient pool produces changes of $+0.010$, $-0.016$, and $-0.031$. These descriptive differences indicate classifier dependence rather than a consistently superior strategy.

The cross-validation implementation reuses synthetic pools trained on all 193 training patients. Fully nested generation and preprocessing are required to eliminate validation-fold information from the augmented training data. The reported mean plus or minus 1.96 fold standard deviations is not a confidence interval for the mean; fold means and standard deviations are retained only as exploratory summaries.

McNemar's test assesses paired errors, not AUC or C-index differences. Overlapping separate bootstrap intervals are likewise not a formal paired comparison. Future inference should use paired AUC and survival-performance procedures, repeated splits, and an explicit multiple-comparison strategy.

FID compares moments of 11 continuous variables; it does not establish multivariate equivalence or preserve outcome relationships. Near-duplicate rates are empirical screening measures, and the simplified DP-SGD projections do not certify a trained private generator. Transplantation is a competing event; the cause-specific analysis does not directly establish cumulative death risks. These limitations constrain claims of clinical readiness and safe data release.

\subsection{Practical Implications}

Synthetic-data evaluation should specify the intended task and preserve an independent real-patient test cohort. Preprocessing, generation, filtering, and model selection should be fitted within each training fold when cross-validation is used. Distributional summaries should be accompanied by prediction metrics, subgroup analyses, and disclosure assessments.

For this cohort, the evidence supports further study of synthetic-only modelling and the use of partially observed training records. It does not justify selecting consensus filtering as universally preferable or describing a generator as safe for release. Quality controls should be assessed for their effects on rare outcomes and extreme but valid biomarker values as well as overall resemblance.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study evaluated whether quality-filtered synthetic records improve prediction for patients with primary biliary cirrhosis, while separating distributional resemblance, empirical disclosure risk, and downstream utility. The evaluation used 276 complete Mayo Clinic records, divided into 193 training patients and 83 held-out test patients. Three core generative architectures were examined with interquartile-range filtering and cross-model consensus, alongside masked-loss VAE, CTGAN, and SMOTE comparisons. Follow-up duration was excluded from baseline predictors, and binary classification was supplemented by three-year classification and time-to-event modelling.

The results demonstrate that synthetic records can retain useful predictive structure without consistently improving models trained on available real data. Synthetic-only cGAN training achieved AUCs of 0.809 for Random Forest and 0.849 for logistic regression, while its Gradient Boosting AUC was 0.687. Consensus filtering yielded 787 records but did not establish a consistent predictive advantage and retained a 21.1\% near-duplicate rate. These findings identify task and classifier dependence as central considerations when selecting synthetic data.

The masked-loss VAE, which incorporated partially observed training records, achieved the lowest tabular Fr\'{e}chet distance of 0.062. Its augmentation raised Random Forest AUC from 0.832 to 0.868; however, the paired accuracy comparison remained non-significant at $p=0.125$. This result motivates further evaluation of incomplete clinical records rather than establishing a confirmed augmentation benefit. Across six synthetic-data methods, fidelity and mean synthetic-only AUC showed an inconclusive association. The limited number of related methods prevents a general conclusion about their relationship.

Several constraints govern interpretation. The test cohort is small, all records originate from one historical dataset, and generative cross-validation reuses pools fitted before fold partitioning. McNemar's tests concern accuracy rather than AUC, and survival-performance differences were not formally tested. Statistical fences can exclude valid extreme observations, while distance-based privacy screening and output perturbation provide no formal disclosure guarantee. Cross-site transfer and IoMT deployment remain prospective applications.

The principal contribution is therefore an empirical framework for examining where synthetic-data quality indicators agree and where they diverge. Future work should regenerate synthetic pools within each validation fold, evaluate paired performance differences, assess competing risks and subgroup calibration, and validate on independent clinical cohorts. Privacy attacks and validated privacy accounting are also required before considering data release. Such evaluation would establish whether the observed model-specific gains persist and whether quality filtering supports the intended clinical task.

Every quantity plotted in S6 to S10 also appears numerically in Tables~\ref{tab:performance} and~\ref{tab:cv}.

Tables~S1 to~S8 of that document report material omitted here for space: the complete architecture and optimiser settings for all three generative models; per-feature Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistics, Jensen-Shannon divergences and Cohen's $d$ for all eleven continuous features across all four synthetic datasets; the full consensus tolerance sweep across eight values; the output-perturbation and DP-SGD privacy sweeps; and the complete subgroup breakdown. Two results there are worth noting. The consensus set records the smallest KS statistics of any method on the hardest biomarkers despite being formally significant on all eleven features, and Cohen's $d$ ranks the VAE as the best-matched method on every feature even though it is the worst on every distributional-shape measure, which is a further instance of the paper's central point that a single fidelity metric can be satisfied by a model that is wrong in every other respect. The supplementary document and figures are distributed with the code repository.

\subsection*{Ethics Statement}
This study used the Mayo Clinic PBC dataset, which is publicly available, fully de-identified, and distributed for research use through the UCI Machine Learning Repository \cite{ucipbc}. No new patient data were collected and no additional ethical approval was required.

\subsection*{Data and Code Availability}
The Mayo Clinic PBC dataset is publicly available from the UCI Machine Learning Repository \cite{ucipbc}. The project provides generative-model, filtering, consensus, and evaluation scripts in Python, with TensorFlow 2.15 used for the core neural generators. Seeds are fixed at 42. Survival analyses use \texttt{lifelines} and \texttt{scikit-survival}. Reproduction requires the corresponding script sequence and software environment; fixed seeds do not ensure identical results across hardware or thread configurations. Code: \url{https://github.com/Michaeludousoro/liver-cirrhosis-synthetic-data}.
